A series premiere is the first episode of a show's first season. A season premiere is the first episode of a later season. A special is an episode that sits outside the regular run, such as a holiday episode or a reunion. These labels are bookkeeping rather than opinion, but they tell you something practical: where it is safe to jump in.

## The labels you will see most often

| Label | What it means | Good place to start? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Series premiere | The first episode of season 1 | Yes. This is the intended front door. |
| Season premiere | The first episode of season 2 or later | Only if you know the earlier seasons, or the show is built from standalone stories. |
| Special | An episode listed outside the normal numbering | Often, but read the description first. |
| Season finale | The last episode of a season | Not as a starting point. |
| Series finale | The last episode of the whole show | Never as a starting point. |

On Cold Open you will see three of these: Series premiere, Season premiere (with the season number, for example "Season 3 premiere") and Special. Finales and mid-season returns are not labeled, because the listings we use do not mark them. If a finale matters to you, the network's own announcement is the place to confirm it.

## A premiere is not always a new show

The word "premiere" causes more misunderstandings than any other label. A "Season 7 premiere" is the return of a show that has been running for years. It is a good night for people who follow the show and a poor one for newcomers, because the episode assumes you know the characters and what happened at the end of last season.

If you see a premiere for a show you have never watched, check the season number first. Season 1 means you are in the right place. Anything higher means you should either go back to the beginning or look up a short recap.

## Where to start: four common cases

**A series premiere of a show that looks interesting.** Start here. Premiere episodes are usually written to introduce the characters and the situation.

**A season premiere of a show you already follow.** Check how the last season ended, and give yourself a minute to remember who was where. Many shows open the new season by answering the cliffhanger from the previous one.

**A season premiere of a show you have never seen.** Look at what kind of show it is. Procedurals, where every episode is a new case, usually let you start anywhere. Anthologies, where each season tells a different story, are often built so that any season works as a first one. Serialized dramas, where one story runs across many episodes, are the ones where starting late hurts.

**A special.** Specials vary a lot. A holiday episode of a comedy is usually a fine first taste. A reunion special for a show that ended years ago will mean little to someone who has not seen it.

## Things that blur the labels

Two details can confuse a listing. Networks sometimes air a "sneak peek": the first episode shown early, before the show's normal time slot. The episode is then repeated in the regular slot, so a show can seem to premiere twice. And premiere nights often include two episodes back to back. Listings count them separately, so the first is labeled as the premiere and the second is simply episode 2.

International dates add another layer. A show that premiered in the United States last year can be a "series premiere" on a British or Australian service this week, because the label depends on the schedule you are looking at.

## How the traditional calendar shapes premieres

American broadcast networks have traditionally started most of their new and returning shows in the autumn, taken a break around the winter holidays, and brought in replacements in the new year. Cable and streaming services follow no such calendar. They release premieres all year, so new series turn up in almost every week. That is the reason a two-week look-ahead is more useful than a seasonal one.

## What the label cannot tell you

A premiere label says nothing about quality. It does not tell you whether the episode is good, whether the show will last, or whether it is available everywhere. It also cannot tell you whether you will like it, which is why the description and the genre tags are worth reading before you commit an hour.
