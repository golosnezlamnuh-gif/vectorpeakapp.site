A listed episode can run late, move to another time or not appear at all, and the most common causes are live events that overrun, local stations changing the plan, holiday breaks and last-minute decisions by the network. Almost none of them are errors in the listing. The schedule shows the plan, and the plan is sometimes changed on the day.

## Live sports run long

The biggest source of late shows is a live sporting event. A game that goes into overtime does not stop at its slot, so everything after it on that channel starts late. Program guides often flag these slots as approximate. If a show is listed after a live game, treat its printed time as the earliest it could begin.

The same applies to award ceremonies, parades and other live events with no fixed end. Anything scheduled after them is a guess.

## Breaking news and special reports

When a major story breaks, networks can interrupt regular programs or replace them. These changes are made within minutes, so no listing can predict them. A recorded show may be shown later in the night, moved to another day or skipped.

## Local stations can make their own choices

Network programs reach most viewers through local stations, and those stations decide what actually airs. A station may preempt a network program, which means replacing it with something else such as local news, a local sports game or a weather warning. In that case a show on your friend's television in another city may not be on yours at the same time. If the network's national listing and your own guide disagree, your own station's guide is the one that applies to you.

## Holidays and planned breaks

Around major holidays the schedule thins out. Networks run specials, reruns or holiday films in place of regular episodes. A planned break in the middle of a season is called a hiatus, and the first episode afterwards is often described as a mid-season return. Hiatuses are usually announced in advance, but the date a show comes back is sometimes confirmed only a few weeks beforehand.

## Network decisions

A network can move a show to a different night, swap two episodes or pull an episode that is not ready. Sometimes this follows production delays. Sometimes it is a response to how another show performed. These changes usually appear in listings within a day, but not always at once.

## Listings are edited by people

The schedule on this site comes from TVmaze, a database that is kept up to date by its community. When a network changes a time, someone has to enter it. A change announced in the afternoon might not be in the data until the evening. For that reason, the saved copy of each day's page, which is refreshed once a day, can be a few hours behind a last-minute change.

## How to check before you give up

1. **Look at your own station or service.** The channel's own guide or app is the final word for your area.
2. **Check the show's official page.** Networks and streamers post their schedule changes there.
3. **Check the date and time zone.** A show that seems missing may simply be on at a time you converted wrongly. Our guide to [reading a TV schedule](/guides/how-to-read-a-tv-schedule/) covers the conversions.
4. **Wait ten minutes.** After a live event, a show often starts only a little late.
5. **Tell us.** If a listing on this site looks wrong, report it on the [contact page](/contact/), and we will also pass the fix to TVmaze.

## Make your recorder work for you

If you record programs, set it to record the series rather than the time slot. A series recording follows the show if it moves. Many recorders also let you add extra minutes at the end of a recording, which saves the last scene of a show that follows a sports game. This setting is worth a minute of your time before the next big game.

## What changes least

Streaming releases are the steadiest part of the schedule. A service chooses a release moment and rarely changes it, so these are the safest choice on a night when live events are on. They sit under "All day" on [today's schedule](/) when no exact time is known.
