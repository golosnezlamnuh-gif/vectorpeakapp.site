"S02E05" means season 2, episode 5. That is the whole system in one line, and it works well until you meet a split season, a special or a two-hour finale. This guide explains the numbering that listings use, why the numbers sometimes differ from site to site, and how to avoid watching things in the wrong order.

## Reading the code

Listings, including this site, write an episode as S for season and E for episode, each with at least two digits.

| Code | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| S01E01 | The first episode of the first season |
| S03E10 | The tenth episode of the third season |
| S02 special | A special that belongs to season 2 but has no regular episode number |
| Special | A special that does not belong to a particular season |

The numbers follow the order in which the episodes are or were broadcast. They describe where an episode sits in the show's run, not how good it is or how important it is to the story.

## Why seasons differ so much in length

A season is the batch of episodes a network or service orders and releases as one unit. How big that batch is depends on the kind of show and on where it airs.

- American broadcast networks traditionally ordered around 22 episodes for a drama or comedy to fill the autumn-to-spring season. Many networks now order fewer.
- Cable and streaming shows are often much shorter, commonly somewhere between six and thirteen episodes, and some have fewer.
- British series are often six to eight episodes long.
- Reality and competition shows vary widely, because their length is tied to how many rounds the format needs.

This is why a show can be "in its fourth season" and still be a small commitment, or "in its second season" and have fifty episodes behind it. Check the number of episodes, not just the number of seasons, before deciding how far behind you are.

## Airing order and production order

Episodes are sometimes made in one order and shown in another. A network may hold back an episode that is not ready, move a holiday episode to the right week, or reshuffle the order to start with a stronger one. Listings follow the order of broadcast, which is also the order the show's makers intend you to watch. A "production code" printed in some databases records the order of filming. It is mainly of interest to fans.

## Double episodes, two-parters and long finales

A two-hour season opener or finale is sometimes listed as one episode and sometimes as two. A story told over two connected episodes, often called a two-parter, usually gets two numbers. The same program can therefore be "S04E09" in one place and "S04E09 and E10" in another, and both can be correct. If the numbers in two places differ by one, this is the first thing to check.

## Specials sit outside the count

Holiday episodes, behind-the-scenes programs, reunions and recap episodes are often given a special label rather than a regular number. On this site, an episode that TVmaze marks as a special is labeled "Special". Some other databases file the same programs under a "Season 0". Both approaches keep specials from throwing off the numbering of the regular episodes.

## Split seasons

Some seasons are released in two halves with a long break in between. The makers may call them "Part 1" and "Part 2" of one season, or they may count them as two seasons. Many databases keep the single season and number the episodes straight through, so Part 2 begins at episode 9 or 10 instead of episode 1. Others restart the count. If you are following a split season, rely on the dates: the next episode is the one that airs next, whatever its number.

## Why two sites can disagree

When the numbers differ between sources, it is almost always one of five things:

1. One source counts a special as a regular episode and the other does not.
2. A two-part episode is counted once in one place and twice in another.
3. A split season is a single season in one database and two seasons in the other.
4. One source uses broadcast order and the other uses production order.
5. A mistake has been made, which will be corrected in time if the source is community-edited.

In all five cases, the episode itself is the same. Use the title and the air date to confirm which one you are looking at.

## Long-running shows and daytime programs

Soap operas, talk shows and other programs that air nearly every day are often tracked by date rather than by season and episode number, because a "season" means little when a show airs for fifty weeks a year. Some long-running series, especially animation, are also counted with an "absolute" number that simply keeps rising across all seasons.

## What to do with this

When you decide where to start a show, look at the number of episodes in season 1, not only at how many seasons exist. When you follow a show, rely on the air dates and the titles. And when you see a code such as S02E05 on [today's schedule](/), read it as an address rather than a ranking.
