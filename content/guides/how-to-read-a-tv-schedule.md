A TV listing is almost always written in the time zone of the network's main feed, which for American networks is usually Eastern Time. To know when you can actually watch, find the zone the time is written in, then add or subtract the difference between that zone and yours. Most schedule confusion comes from skipping that first step.

## Start with the zone label

Look for a label next to the time: ET for Eastern, CT for Central, MT for Mountain, PT for Pacific. A line such as "Thursday, 9 p.m. ET" is exact. If a listing has no label at all, assume Eastern for a US network and local time for a British one, because British listings use the clock in London.

When a listing shows more than one time, the first number is nearly always the Eastern one. That is where the odd-looking "8/7c" notation comes from.

## What "8/7c" means

You will see it at the end of trailers and on-screen promos: "Sundays at 8/7c". It is a compact way to give two times at once.

- The number before the slash is the time in the Eastern and Pacific zones.
- The number after the slash, followed by a "c", is the time in the Central zone.

So "8/7c" means 8 p.m. in New York and Los Angeles and 7 p.m. in Chicago. The reason is that the big networks send out one feed for the East Coast and a second one for the West Coast, and the West Coast feed shows the same program three hours later on the clock. Viewers in the Central zone get the East Coast feed one hour earlier on their clocks. Mountain time is the awkward one, because it depends on the local station, so check your own station's guide.

## Live shows are different

The two-feed arrangement is for taped programs: dramas, comedies and most reality shows. Live events air at the same moment everywhere. A live awards show that starts at 8 p.m. Eastern starts at 5 p.m. in Los Angeles, and nobody sees a delayed West Coast version. Sports and news work the same way. If a listing says "live", read the time as a single moment in time and convert it, rather than expecting a second showing.

## Convert a listed time in ten seconds

Start from Eastern time and move one hour per zone:

| Listed in ET | Central | Mountain | Pacific |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 7 p.m. | 6 p.m. | 5 p.m. | 4 p.m. |
| 8 p.m. | 7 p.m. | 6 p.m. | 5 p.m. |
| 9 p.m. | 8 p.m. | 7 p.m. | 6 p.m. |
| 10 p.m. | 9 p.m. | 8 p.m. | 7 p.m. |

That table is for a live event or a single listed moment. For a taped program on the West Coast feed, use the same clock number as the East Coast, as in the "8/7c" example above.

For viewers outside the United States, the gap between New York and London is normally five hours. It shrinks to four hours for a few weeks in March and for about a week at the end of October, because the US and the UK change their clocks on different dates. This catches people out every year.

## Streaming works differently

A streaming episode is not "on" at a time. It is released at a moment, and from then on you can watch whenever you like. The release moment is one instant for the whole world, so it lands on different clock times in different places. Some services have long released new episodes around midnight Pacific, which is 3 a.m. Eastern. Others publish in the evening of the release day. The service's own release schedule is the final word.

If you live far from the United States, the date can move as well. An episode released on Friday evening in Los Angeles is already Saturday in much of Europe. That is why Cold Open puts streaming releases without a fixed time under "All day" and shows the times it does know in your own time zone.

## Clock changes and late-night listings

US clocks go forward on the second Sunday of March and back on the first Sunday of November. A program "at 9 p.m. ET" stays at 9 p.m. Eastern all year, but the number of hours between you and the broadcaster may change for a few weeks. A screenshot of a schedule taken last month can be an hour out.

Late-night shows add one more trap: a program listed after midnight belongs to the broadcast day before. A talk show listed at 12:35 a.m. on Friday is the Thursday night lineup.

## A five-second check before you plan your evening

1. Find the zone label, or assume Eastern for US networks.
2. Convert to your own time zone with the table above.
3. Ask whether it is live or taped. Live means one moment for everyone.
4. For streaming, look up the release moment rather than guessing.
5. If the listed time falls after midnight, check which date it belongs to.
